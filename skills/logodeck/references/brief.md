# Brief and discovery

A good logo starts with a decision, not a mood board. The brief exists to make the decision
explicit before anyone draws. Keep it to one page. Put it in your working notes; the final
deliverable is the deck.

## The five questions

Ask at most five. Stop as soon as you can write a defensible brief.

1. **What is the name, exactly?** Spelling, capitalisation, diacritics, abbreviation, and how
   it is pronounced.
2. **What does it promise, in one sentence a customer would recognise?** Not the mission
   statement; the useful outcome.
3. **Who must recognise it, in what context?** Buyers, users, partners, regulators, a store
   shelf, a browser tab, an app launcher.
4. **What must it avoid?** Competitor look, category cliché, a colour that means something
   else locally, a symbol with a protected or sensitive reading.
5. **What already exists?** Name, domain, old mark, typeface, colour, templates, a deck that
   must stay compatible.

If the user answers only part of this, fill the rest with assumptions. Write them in one
short block:

```
Assumptions: B2B buyers; English-first with French accents later; no existing mark;
must work as a 16 px favicon; no budget for a licensed typeface, so the wordmark is drawn.
```

Assumptions are not a weakness. Hidden assumptions are.

## Brief template

```
Name:
Promise:            one sentence
Audience:           who, where, how often they see it
Adjectives:         three to five, each with an opposite
Competitors:        two or three, and what they look like
Category codes:     what buyers expect to see
Clichés to avoid:   what everyone draws, and why we will not
Constraints:        colour, format, language, legacy, accessibility
Success test:       how we will know the mark works
Out of scope:       trademark clearance, naming, domain, campaign
```

The adjectives are useful only if they change a shape. "Trustworthy" changes nothing.
"Anchored, not heavy" changes the amount of weight at the bottom. "Precise, not clinical"
changes the corner language. Pair every adjective with its opposite; the tension is the design.

## Category codes versus clichés

The category code is what makes a mark legible to a buyer. The cliché is the same code used
literally. Keep the code, reject the literal.

| Category | Code buyers expect | Cliché to avoid | A better angle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sustainability | green, growth, land | a leaf, a globe, a recycling loop | a contour, a seed crack, a tool mark |
| Security | enclosure, solidity | a shield, a padlock, a check | a keyway, a seam, a guarded gap |
| Finance | stability, movement | a rising arrow, a coin, a chart | a ledger rule, a balance point, a knot |
| Health | care, calm, precision | a heart, a cross, a pulse line | a breath curve, a stitch, a soft notch |
| Developer tools | precision, speed | angle brackets, a terminal, a bolt | a gate, a commit fork, a bracket joint |
| Food | craft, origin | a fork, a chef hat, a leaf | a crumb, a field row, a cut |
| Education | growth, openness | an open book, a graduation cap | a margin, a page corner, a step |
| Logistics | movement, reliability | a truck, an arrow, a route pin | a seam, a latch, a stacked plane |
| Culture | expression, place | a mask, a skyline, a brush | a rhythm, a fold, a local glyph |
| AI | intelligence, connection | a brain, a sparkle, a robot | a lattice, a signal gap, a lens |

Use the table as a prompt, not as a rule. The best mark often borrows a code from a neighbouring
category and brings it home.

## Word map

Write six columns. Do not filter yet.

1. **Literal nouns** — what the company sells, makes, moves, stores.
2. **Verbs** — what the customer does with it.
3. **Materials and tools** — what it is physically made of or used with.
4. **Adjacent worlds** — a craft, a landscape, a machine, a game with the same rhythm.
5. **Opposites** — the opposite of the promise, used as a shape constraint.
6. **Local and sensory** — a place, a sound, a texture, a word in the user's language.

Then draw lines between two columns. "Seed" + "contour" produced the Loam example. "Latch" +
"seam" is a logistics mark. "Margin" + "step" is an education mark. Two concrete nouns from
different columns usually beat one abstract noun.

## Positioning

Place the brand on two axes that matter to the buyer, not to the designer. Examples:

- traditional ←→ experimental
- mass ←→ specialist
- quiet ←→ loud
- local ←→ global
- handmade ←→ engineered

Mark the three competitors. Mark the brand. The empty space on the map is a direction; the
crowded space is a warning. Do not invent a position the product cannot keep.

## Research without pretending

If web search is available, check current marks in the category, recent rebrands, and local
symbols with a second meaning. Save one line per finding: what it looks like, what it repeats,
what is missing.

If web search is not available, say "no live research" in the final report. Use the brief, the
trope table and your own knowledge. Do not claim a competitive review you did not do.

## Brief smells

- More than five adjectives: the brand has no position yet.
- "Premium" with no proof: it changes the price, not the shape.
- A mascot requested before the mark works in one colour.
- A symbol that only makes sense with the tagline.
- A colour chosen because a competitor uses it.
- A name that cannot be spelled after hearing it once.
- A request to "make it like [famous brand]" — see the copy rule in the main skill.

## Output of this step

A one-page brief, a list of six to ten category tropes to avoid, and a word map with at least
three promising two-word combinations. Move to `marks.md` to choose what kind of mark those
combinations want to become.
